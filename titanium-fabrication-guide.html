Titanium Fabrication Essentials: A Shop-Floor Guide from Cutting to Assembly

Titanium's reputation for corrosion resistance and high strength often creates a false impression that it is easy to fabricate. The opposite is true: poor thermal conductivity, rapid work hardening and high chemical reactivity at temperature make titanium a difficult metal to process. When titanium equipment fails, the cause is usually the fabrication route, not the material.

Fabrication 12 min read Updated November 2026

This guide follows the real shop-floor sequence — cutting, machining, forming, welding, heat treatment, surface treatment and assembly — and sets out the key controls at each step. It complements our guides on titanium industrial selection and on troubleshooting titanium problems in service.

The content is general process knowledge for buyers and engineers discussing requirements with a fabricator. Specific parameters must be set by a qualified fabricator for the grade, product form, drawing and applicable standard.

1. Cutting: start clean from the first cut

Cutting looks simple, yet it is the first entry point for iron contamination and thermal damage.

  • Choose the method: sawing, waterjet, laser and plasma are all usable, but oxy-fuel and ordinary oxy-acetylene cutting are not suitable for titanium — high-temperature oxidation contaminates the cut and titanium swarf presents a fire risk.
  • Leave stock for the heat-affected zone: laser and plasma cutting leave an oxidised, hardened edge. Subsequent machining must remove this layer completely; no 'black skin' may enter the next operation.
  • Collect swarf separately: fine titanium swarf and powder are combustible. Use dedicated containers, keep them away from ignition sources, sort by grade and never mix with carbon-steel swarf.
  • Isolate tooling: platforms, slings and wire ropes that have touched carbon steel must be cleaned or padded before contacting titanium.

2. Machining: neither too slow nor too fast

Titanium conducts heat at roughly one quarter the rate of steel, so cutting heat concentrates at the tool tip. It also work-hardens readily and can diffuse into tool materials at temperature. These three traits give titanium machining its unusual behaviour.

  • Tooling: use carbide (YG-class tungsten-cobalt) or coated tools with a sharp, wear-resistant edge; avoid prolonged cutting with high-speed-steel tools.
  • Cutting parameters: low cutting speed, generous feed and adequate depth of cut — let the tool cut rather than rub. Too shallow a cut and repeated rubbing cause work hardening, making the surface progressively harder.
  • Never dwell mid-cut: do not let the tool dwell or rub against the workpiece; this instantly forms a hardened layer that will damage the tool on the next pass.
  • Ample cooling: use high-pressure coolant to remove heat and flush chips; dry cutting is only for a few special tooling cases.
  • Drilling and tapping: use carbide drills, maintain continuous feed without frequent withdrawal, and use coated taps with good lubrication; slightly enlarge tapping drill size to compensate for springback.

3. Forming: remember springback, iron and heat

  • Springback is significant: titanium's elastic modulus is about half that of steel, so bending and stamping spring back far more; tooling design must compensate. Commercially pure titanium (Gr.1/Gr.2) forms well at room temperature; alpha-beta alloys such as Gr.5 (Ti-6Al-4V) are difficult to cold form and usually need hot forming.
  • Minimum bend radius matters: excessive bending cracks the outer fibre; set the radius by grade and thickness.
  • Control temperature and atmosphere: hold hot-forming temperatures strictly by grade; excessive heat causes oxidation, hydrogen pickup and grain growth. Use inert atmosphere or rapid heating where possible and anneal after forming as required.
  • Prevent iron contamination throughout: rust or carbon-steel particles on dies, rollers and platforms press iron into the surface and seed later pitting.

4. Welding: the highest-risk step for titanium equipment

Titanium is extremely sensitive to oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen, and reactivity rises sharply above 400 °C. Welding is the step where compromise is least acceptable.

  • High-purity argon: GTAW/TIG welding must use argon of at least 99.99% purity.
  • Shield both faces: not just the torch side — the back of the weld, inside the tube, must also be purged with argon, with a trailing shield protecting the solidified bead. Do not remove the gas until the weld and heat-affected zone have cooled below 200 °C. Lack of back purging is the most common site failure cause.
  • Extreme cleanliness: within 50 mm either side of the joint there must be no oil, moisture, scale or marker ink; filler wire must be wiped clean too.
  • Dedicated tools: use titanium-only stainless wire brushes or clean grinding wheels; never share tools with carbon steel.
  • Control heat input and interpass temperature: avoid overheating that coarsens grain and reduces toughness.
  • Judge by weld colour: silver-white is acceptable; yellow, blue, grey and black indicate progressive oxidation and must be ground out and rewelded. This is the most direct quality indicator.
  • Qualification first: titanium welding differs from carbon-steel welding. A qualified WPS/PQR and certified welders should be in place before release. For site repair without a qualified welder and proper shielding, return the piece to the workshop rather than weld under inadequate conditions.

Buyer checklist: ask who issued the WPS/PQR, whether welders are certified, how back purging is implemented, and how weld colour is judged and recorded. Ownership of these documents and records must be confirmed per order; they are not automatically issued by ALLOYTI.

5. Heat treatment: prevent oxidation and hydrogen pickup

  • Stress relief: stress-relief annealing after welding and cold work reduces residual stress and the risk of stress corrosion cracking (SCC).
  • Protective atmosphere: anneal in vacuum or inert atmosphere. Heating in a normal air furnace forms scale and may cause hydrogen pickup, so a subsequent pickling step must be allowed for.
  • Temperature window: commercially pure titanium is typically annealed in the several-hundred-degree Celsius range, strictly by grade, to avoid grain coarsening and loss of strength and ductility.
  • Avoid hydrogen embrittlement: wet processes such as pickling and plating can introduce hydrogen; affected parts need vacuum dehydrogenation, or ductility drops and brittle fracture becomes possible.

6. Surface treatment and cleaning: protect the passive film

Titanium's corrosion resistance depends entirely on the dense TiO₂ passive film on its surface. Fabrication is, in essence, the process of keeping that film intact.

  • Iron contamination is the primary enemy: carbon-steel brushes, hammers, iron platforms and wire ropes embed iron particles in the titanium surface, forming an Fe–Ti galvanic cell that develops into pitting in chloride media. Titanium-only tooling and physical separation from carbon-steel work areas are hard requirements.
  • Detect iron contamination: use the ferroxyl (potassium ferricyanide) test and treat any colour change.
  • Pickle and passivate: a nitric-plus-hydrofluoric acid solution removes scale and iron contamination and reactivates the passive film; rinse thoroughly afterwards, since residual acid causes later corrosion.
  • Never use carbon-steel wire brushes or ordinary grinding wheels on titanium; blasting media must be clean and iron-free.
  • Avoid scratches and contamination: use nylon slings or padding for transport and lifting; isolate temporary fixtures and supports from the titanium.

7. Assembly: keep crevices and galvanic couples out

  • Insulate dissimilar metals: direct contact between titanium and metals with very different potentials, such as carbon steel or copper, accelerates local corrosion in conductive media such as seawater. Use insulating gaskets and sleeves.
  • Reduce crevices: flange faces and bolted laps are high-risk zones for chloride concentration and crevice corrosion. Prefer continuous welds over bolted laps where practical; centre gaskets and match their specification.
  • Tighten evenly: tighten in diagonal sequence to the specified torque in stages, avoiding eccentric loading, local stress concentration and leakage.
  • Prevent arc damage: do not strike or flash an arc on titanium surfaces during nearby welding; even a small arc mark can become a crack initiation site.

8. Safety: titanium swarf is not ordinary metal swarf

Fine titanium powder and swarf are combustible in air and present a fire and explosion risk at sufficient concentration near an ignition source. A fabrication area must use dedicated containers, keep swarf away from fire and heat, sort it by grade, prohibit mixing with ordinary metal swarf and avoid raising dust during cleanup.

Conclusion: clean, protect, control temperature

Titanium fabrication comes down to three rules: clean — dedicated tooling and zero tolerance of iron contamination; protect — full argon shielding throughout welding and an intact passive film throughout processing; control temperature — do not let titanium run unprotected at uncontrolled heat in cutting, forming, welding or heat treatment.

Write these three rules into cutting lists, process cards and work instructions, and titanium equipment can be made right the first time and stay reliable. Remember: titanium failures are, more often than not, process failures rather than material failures.
