Titanium Heat Exchanger Tubes: A Practical Specification Guide

A heat exchanger tube is a small part with a long list of decisions behind it. This guide walks through the choices that decide whether a titanium tube performs in service — the medium, the standard, the manufacturing route, the wall, the ends and the documents.

Tubes 11 min read Updated November 2026

Titanium tube is specified when chloride, seawater, process condensate or a oxidising acid environment makes copper alloy or stainless steel tube unreliable. The decision is rarely about the material alone: the grade, manufacturing route, wall thickness, end preparation and inspection scope all have to be written into the same order.

This guide is written for engineers and buyers who need to describe a tube clearly enough for a supplier to quote it — and clear enough that the delivered material can be checked against what was agreed.

Start with the service, not with the grade

Titanium resists many chloride-bearing waters, seawater, wet chlorine and oxidising acids because of a thin, stable oxide film. That film is useful only when the chemistry supports it. Reducing acids, dry chlorine, high-temperature crevices and heavily fouled deposits need a separate corrosion review before the grade is fixed.

Write down the medium and concentration, continuous and peak temperature, pressure, flow velocity, cleaning method and whether the tube will be in a crevice or under deposit. A grade chosen without those values is a name, not a specification.

Rule of thumb: if the medium is not fully understood, do not buy titanium tube on a grade name alone. Share the service conditions with your corrosion engineer and the supplier before committing to material.

Standards and grades to name on the order

ASTM B338/B338M is the common specification for seamless and welded titanium and titanium-alloy condenser and heat exchanger tubes. ASME SB338 is the code adoption used in many pressure-equipment projects. Enhanced-surface tubes, where the surface is cold-formed to improve heat transfer, are covered by ASTM B891/B891M; the two standards should not be mixed on one order without agreement.

Name one base standard, and name any enhanced-surface requirement separately.

Grade 2 unalloyed titanium is the usual starting point for seawater and chloride service. Grade 1 offers lower strength but more formability; Grade 7 and Grade 12 are considered where crevice corrosion or specific reducing conditions are a concern; Grade 9 is an alloy option when higher strength is needed. Grade availability and exact chemistry follow the standard edition named on the order.

Availability is confirmed per order. A grade being listed in a standard does not mean every diameter, wall and condition is available from every mill. Ask for the supply route before you promise a delivery date.

Seamless or welded tube

Both seamless and welded tube are covered by B338/B338M. The choice is driven by diameter, wall, length, quantity and inspection expectation — not by the assumption that one route is always better.

For a welded tube, ask how the seam is inspected and what acceptance standard is used. For a seamless tube, ask about the piercing and drawing route and the delivery condition. In both cases, define what will be measured and documented instead of relying on the word 'seamless' or 'welded' alone.

Diameter, wall thickness and tolerance

Tube orders fail on dimensions more often than on chemistry. Outside diameter and wall thickness control fit in the tube sheet, pressure capability and resistance to collapse during expansion. Length, straightness, ovality and end condition must be stated together.

A dimensional line is complete only when the tolerance and measuring basis are also agreed.

  • State whether wall thickness is nominal or minimum.
  • Confirm whether OD or ID governs the fit at the tube sheet.
  • For U-tubes, give bend radius and straight leg allowance.
  • Ask for the delivery condition: annealed, cold-worked or as agreed.

Do not treat wall thickness as a free parameter. A lighter wall may pass a chemical certificate and still be wrong for the expansion joint or the design pressure. Wall tolerance and inspection scope belong on the same purchase order.

End preparation and the tube-to-tubesheet joint

Most tube-side leakage starts at the joint, not in the middle of the tube. The end may be expanded, welded, or both. The joint design belongs to the exchanger fabricator, but the tube supplier must deliver ends that can be prepared and joined as specified.

  • Expanded joint: confirm expansion range, surface finish and whether a test expansion is required.
  • Welded joint: confirm bevel, projection length, cleaning and welding procedure.
  • Combined joint: state the sequence — weld then expand, or expand then weld — agreed with the fabricator.
  • Enhanced-surface tube: the ends are normally left unenhanced for rolling-in, as described in ASTM B891/B891M.

Keep the end specification with the tube order. If the exchanger will be expanded into the tube sheet, a bright, clean, correctly sized end is part of the material requirement — not a site detail to discover after delivery.

Inspection, testing and documents

A certificate is a record, not a performance guarantee. Define what will be inspected, which test method is used, and which document will be issued. For enhanced-surface tube, ASTM B891/B891M states that each tube is subject to a nondestructive eddy current test and either a pneumatic or hydrostatic test. For plain tube to B338/B338M, the test scope follows the standard and the order.

Failure modes worth designing out

When a titanium tube leaks, the cause is usually local rather than a general shortage of corrosion resistance. Understanding the pattern helps the next specification avoid the same problem.

  • Crevice corrosion under gaskets, deposits or poor support — controlled by detail design and cleanliness, not by guessing a higher grade.
  • Pitting in hot chloride service — review temperature and deposit conditions before switching grade.
  • Erosion or thinning at the inlet end — review velocity, impingement and tube inlet protection.
  • Vibration at support plates — review spacing and straightness, not only material.
  • Galvanic pairing with less noble metals — insulate or isolate where required.

None of these failure modes is solved by a certificate alone. The material specification, exchanger design and operating practice have to agree. If you are replacing failed tubes, send the failed sample history together with the original tube order — it usually shortens the next specification conversation.

A shorter path from enquiry to quotation

A usable tube enquiry does not need every answer on the first day, but it should separate what is fixed from what is still being confirmed. The list below is enough to start a technical response.

  • Product and standard: condenser or heat exchanger tube; ASTM B338/B338M, ASME SB338 or enhanced surface.
  • Grade and condition: for example Gr.2, annealed; state any alternative being considered.
  • Dimensions: OD, wall (nominal or minimum), cut length or U-tube geometry.
  • Quantity and destination: total length or piece count, port and target delivery.
  • Joint and ends: expanded, welded or combined; end finish and projection.
  • Service conditions: medium, concentration, temperature, pressure and cleaning method.
  • Documents: certificate type, NDT, pressure test, marking and any third-party inspection.
